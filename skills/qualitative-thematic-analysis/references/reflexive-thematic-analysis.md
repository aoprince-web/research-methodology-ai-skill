# Reflexive thematic analysis: practical guide

## Orientation

Braun and Clarke's reflexive thematic analysis is an interpretive approach to developing patterns of shared meaning across a dataset. The researcher is active in generating codes and developing themes. It is not a neutral process in which themes are waiting in the data to be discovered.

The approach must fit the study's theoretical and epistemological assumptions. Do not apply the label solely because interviews were conducted or because the analysis has six headings.

## The six recursive phases

### 1. Familiarisation

Read and re-read the dataset. Listen to recordings where available and permitted. Make notes about context, initial patterns, tensions and questions. Familiarisation is more than skimming or summarising each transcript.

### 2. Coding

Code data relevant to the research question. Codes may capture explicit meanings or more interpretive assumptions and implications. Keep enough context to understand each extract. Coding is flexible and evolves as the researcher engages with the dataset.

### 3. Generating initial themes

Bring codes and data extracts together around a potential shared meaning. Ask what the pattern says, rather than merely what topic it concerns. A candidate theme needs a central organising concept.

### 4. Developing and reviewing themes

Examine the fit and coherence of extracts within each theme, then consider how the theme works in relation to the dataset as a whole. Look for overlap, gaps, tensions and material that challenges the interpretation. Revise the thematic structure where needed.

### 5. Refining, defining and naming themes

Define the scope and central concept of each theme. Give it a concise name that communicates an analytic point. Subthemes should identify meaningful dimensions of a larger pattern, not simply divide a theme into smaller topics.

### 6. Writing

Construct a coherent analytic account. Use selected extracts to illustrate and substantiate the interpretation. Explain how each extract contributes to the analytic claim. Writing is part of analysis, not merely a final presentation task.

## Reflexivity

Use analytic memos to consider:
- What assumptions am I bringing to this account?
- Why did I attend to this extract?
- How does my role, experience or relationship to the setting shape my reading?
- What other plausible interpretation exists?
- Which voices or perspectives may be less visible in the dataset?
- How has my interpretation changed?

Reflexivity should be specific to the project, not a generic paragraph added at the end.

## What not to impose

Do not automatically require:
- a fixed codebook;
- independent coding by multiple researchers;
- inter-rater reliability statistics;
- consensus as proof of a correct code;
- saturation as a universal stopping rule;
- member checking as a universal validation test.

Such procedures may be appropriate in other approaches or specific designs, but should not be imported without methodological justification.

## Reporting

Describe the analytic approach accurately, explain how it was used in this study, and show the reasoning from data extracts to themes. Avoid claiming that the six phases were followed mechanically or that the analysis is objective simply because a procedure was documented.
