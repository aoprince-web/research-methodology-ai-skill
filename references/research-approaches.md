# Research Approaches: A Practical Selection Guide

This note provides an original planning aid for choosing between qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods approaches. The decision should be justified by the research problem and questions, not by a preference for a particular method.

## Start with the kind of answer needed

| If the main purpose is to… | An approach to consider | Questions to resolve |
|---|---|---|
| Understand meanings, experiences, perspectives, or processes in context | Qualitative | Whose perspectives matter? What context is needed? What depth of evidence is feasible? |
| Estimate levels, describe distributions, test specified relationships, compare groups, or measure change | Quantitative | What is being measured? How are variables operationalised? What sample and analysis are appropriate? |
| Answer linked questions that require both numerical patterns and contextual explanation | Mixed methods | Why are both strands necessary? How will they be sequenced and integrated? |

These are starting points, not automatic rules. A research question may be refined as the researcher learns more about the problem and available evidence.

## Qualitative approach

A qualitative approach is appropriate when the study needs detailed, contextual evidence about meaning, experience, interpretation, interaction, or process. Common sources include interviews, observations, documents, and artefacts.

A proposal should state:
- what experience, process, or meaning will be investigated;
- why qualitative evidence is suitable;
- which qualitative design or analytic tradition will guide the work;
- who or what can provide relevant evidence;
- how the material will be collected and analysed;
- how reflexivity, trustworthiness, ethics, and context will be addressed.

Do not equate qualitative research with small samples alone. Its defining logic concerns the questions, evidence, analysis, and claims.

## Quantitative approach

A quantitative approach is appropriate when the study requires numerical description, measurement, comparison, or statistical analysis. A proposal should define constructs and variables, explain how they will be measured, identify the population and sampling strategy, and connect each question to a suitable analysis.

Consider whether the purpose is descriptive, associational, predictive, evaluative, or causal. These purposes imply different design requirements. In particular, a statistical association is not automatically a causal effect.

## Mixed methods approach

Mixed methods is useful when the research purpose requires complementary forms of evidence and a meaningful integration plan. The researcher should state:
- why one strand alone would not adequately answer the problem;
- what each strand contributes;
- which strand comes first, or whether they occur in parallel;
- whether one strand has priority;
- where and how results will be integrated;
- how convergent and conflicting findings will be interpreted.

Collecting a survey and conducting interviews does not, by itself, explain the logic of a mixed methods design.

## Worldviews and assumptions

Research involves assumptions about what can be known, how knowledge is developed, what counts as evidence, and how values relate to inquiry. A worldview or philosophical position should be discussed when it helps explain the design and decisions. Avoid adding philosophical terminology as decoration. Explain its practical implications for the questions, researcher role, evidence, and interpretation.

## Feasibility screen

Before recommending a design, assess:
- access to participants, cases, documents, or datasets;
- the time required for approval, recruitment, collection, and analysis;
- researcher skills and software access;
- financial and logistical resources;
- language and cultural considerations;
- participant burden and ethical risks;
- the likely scope of defensible conclusions.

If a design is not feasible, explain what would need to change or suggest a narrower question. Do not quietly replace the researcher's aim with a different one.
