# Mixed Methods Design and Integration Guide

Mixed methods research deliberately combines qualitative and quantitative approaches to address a research problem. The design should explain why both forms of evidence are needed and how they will be brought together.

## 1. Justify the need for both strands

Ask:
- What can the quantitative strand establish?
- What can the qualitative strand illuminate?
- Why is one strand alone insufficient for the study's purpose?
- What additional understanding is expected from integration?

If the two strands answer unrelated questions and no integration is planned, reconsider whether the label “mixed methods” is justified.

## 2. Choose a design that matches the purpose

| Design pattern | Sequence | Typical rationale | Main planning task |
|---|---|---|---|
| Convergent | Strands occur in a related timeframe | Compare or combine different forms of evidence | Specify how results will be compared and interpreted |
| Explanatory sequential | Quantitative, then qualitative | Explain or elaborate on quantitative patterns | Decide which results need follow-up and how participants or cases will be selected |
| Exploratory sequential | Qualitative, then quantitative | Explore concepts or develop a measure, model, or later test | Explain how qualitative findings inform the later strand |
| Multiphase | Several linked stages | Address a broader programme of connected questions | Explain how stages build on one another and where integration occurs |

These are broad patterns. The actual design should be described in enough detail to make the sequence and logic clear.

## 3. Set priority and timing

State whether the strands have equal priority or one has a primary role, and explain why. Specify the timing and dependencies, including whether the first strand must be analysed before the second can be designed or recruited.

## 4. Plan integration explicitly

Integration may occur by:
- **connecting:** results from one strand guide sampling or data collection in another;
- **building:** findings from one strand inform the instrument or procedure used in another;
- **merging:** results are compared or combined during interpretation;
- **embedding:** one strand is placed within a broader design or intervention.

Name the actual procedure that will be used. A statement that “the data will be triangulated” is insufficient without describing how comparison will work.

A joint display or comparison matrix can help show where results converge, complement one another, or diverge. Conflicting results should be investigated and reported, not hidden to create an artificial agreement.

## 5. Check feasibility

Mixed methods can require more time, recruitment, skills, and resources than a single-strand study. Estimate the work for each strand separately and for integration. If the project is short, consider narrowing the questions or using a clearly justified design with a realistic integration plan.

## 6. Reporting checklist

- [ ] Rationale for using both strands is explicit.
- [ ] Each strand has a clear purpose and suitable design.
- [ ] Sequence and priority are stated.
- [ ] Sampling and data collection are described for each strand.
- [ ] Analysis is described for each strand.
- [ ] The point and method of integration are explicit.
- [ ] Convergent and divergent findings will be considered.
- [ ] Integrated conclusions do not exceed the evidence.
