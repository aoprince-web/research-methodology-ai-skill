# Qualitative Methods: Design, Collection, Analysis, and Quality

This is an original working guide informed by qualitative research-methodology literature. The appropriate procedures depend on the selected tradition, research question, setting, and institutional requirements.

## 1. Design before technique

Begin with the intended understanding or analytic outcome. Interviews are a collection method, not a research design by themselves. Choose and justify a design that fits the purpose.

| Design | Typical focus | Key planning question |
|---|---|---|
| Basic qualitative study | How people interpret experiences or construct meaning | What understanding of the issue is needed? |
| Phenomenology | Meaning or structure of lived experience | What experience is being examined, and which phenomenological tradition will guide the work? |
| Grounded theory | Developing an explanatory account or theory of a process | How will data collection and analysis interact as the explanation develops? |
| Ethnography | Shared practices, meanings, and social patterns | What group or setting will be studied, and what engagement is feasible? |
| Narrative inquiry | Stories, life histories, and the organisation of experience | How will stories be collected and interpreted in context? |
| Qualitative case study | In-depth investigation of a bounded case | What defines the case, its boundaries, and the evidence sources? |

These categories are not interchangeable. Use the terminology and procedures of the chosen tradition accurately.

## 2. Purposeful sampling

Qualitative selection is generally driven by relevance and information needs rather than statistical representation. Possible strategies include criterion-based selection, maximum variation, typical or critical cases, and snowball recruitment. Select a strategy because it fits the question and access conditions.

State:
- the inclusion and exclusion criteria;
- how potential participants or cases will be identified;
- why their experience or position is relevant;
- how diversity or variation matters to the study;
- how recruitment will stop or be reviewed;
- foreseeable access and selection limitations.

Do not state that a fixed interview count automatically guarantees saturation. Explain the rationale used to judge the adequacy and richness of the material.

## 3. Interviews

An interview guide should connect to the research questions while leaving room for participants to describe what matters to them. Use open, clear, non-leading questions. Avoid questions that assume a cause, judgement, or expected answer.

A practical sequence is:
1. brief introduction and consent confirmation;
2. accessible opening question;
3. questions about relevant experience or context;
4. focused questions linked to the study aims;
5. neutral probes for detail or clarification;
6. a closing invitation to add anything important;
7. explanation of next steps and contact arrangements, where appropriate.

Plan the interview mode, language, duration, recording, note-taking, transcription, storage, and privacy protections. Pilot the guide when appropriate and revise confusing or leading items.

## 4. Observation

Define what will be observed and why observation is needed. Specify the setting, researcher role, access, duration or schedule, field-note process, and how the researcher's presence may influence behaviour. Address consent and privacy, particularly where people may not reasonably expect to be observed.

Separate descriptive notes about what was seen or heard from reflective notes about interpretation, reactions, and emerging questions. This distinction helps make the analytic process more transparent.

## 5. Documents and artefacts

Explain which documents or artefacts are relevant and how they will be selected. Consider authorship, purpose, intended audience, context, date, completeness, authenticity, and access restrictions. A document is produced for a purpose; do not treat it as a neutral or complete record of reality.

## 6. Analysis

Use an analytic process that fits the study's design. Common activities may include:
- familiarisation with the material;
- organising and documenting the dataset;
- coding meaningful segments;
- comparing codes and cases;
- developing categories, patterns, or themes;
- writing analytic memos;
- examining exceptions and contradictory evidence;
- interpreting findings in relation to the question and literature;
- selecting excerpts that genuinely support the reported interpretation.

Do not describe analysis as a mechanical procedure that guarantees an answer. Explain the researcher's role in interpretation. Do not invent data excerpts or present illustrative examples as real participant material.

## 7. Trustworthiness and reflexivity

Quality strategies should fit the study rather than being added as a checklist without explanation. Depending on the design, the researcher may consider:
- **Credibility:** whether interpretations are well supported by the evidence and context.
- **Dependability:** whether the research process is documented clearly enough to understand how decisions were made.
- **Confirmability:** whether interpretations are grounded in the data and the researcher's influence is considered.
- **Transferability:** whether sufficient contextual detail is provided for readers to judge relevance elsewhere.
- **Reflexivity:** how the researcher's position, relationships, assumptions, and decisions shape the inquiry.

Triangulation, participant feedback, peer discussion, audit trails, and rich description can be useful in some circumstances. None is a universal guarantee of quality. Explain why a chosen strategy fits and how it will be carried out.

## 8. Ethical practice

Consider voluntary participation, informed consent, confidentiality, privacy, emotional discomfort, power relationships, gatekeeper influence, secure data management, and the possibility that a small or distinctive setting makes participants identifiable. Ethics is not only an approval form; it affects recruitment, collection, interpretation, and reporting.
