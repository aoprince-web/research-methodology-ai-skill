# Literature Review and Research Gap Guide

A literature review should build a reasoned account of what is known, how it is known, where findings differ, and what remains unresolved for the proposed study. Its structure should support the research problem rather than becoming a list of article summaries.

## 1. Define the review's job

Before searching, clarify whether the review is intended to:
- establish the context and rationale for a new study;
- map the extent and nature of literature on a topic;
- synthesise findings across studies;
- assess evidence for a focused question;
- develop a theoretical or conceptual framing;
- identify how previous studies have defined or measured a concept.

The purpose determines the appropriate breadth, search procedure, and level of documentation.

## 2. Build a search plan

Record:
- key concepts and synonyms;
- relevant databases, catalogues, repositories, and other source types;
- search strings and dates;
- date, language, population, setting, or design limits and the reasons for them;
- inclusion and exclusion criteria;
- how records will be screened and selected.

Do not describe a limited or informal search as comprehensive. If the project is a systematic or scoping review, use the reporting and methodological standards appropriate to that review type.

## 3. Evaluate sources

For each source, consider its relevance, purpose, design, setting, sample or corpus, measures or data sources, analysis, findings, limitations, and contribution to the proposed study. Distinguish peer-reviewed research from policy reports, working papers, theses, news, and other sources where the distinction affects how evidence should be interpreted.

Do not assume that publication alone makes a claim reliable. Do not cite a source for a claim it does not support.

## 4. Synthesis rather than summary

Organise the review around themes, debates, concepts, methods, or patterns. A synthesis can explain:
- where findings converge or diverge;
- whether differences may relate to setting, population, design, measures, or time;
- which methods dominate and what they can establish;
- which populations, settings, or perspectives receive limited attention;
- what evidence remains uncertain or contradictory;
- how the proposed study relates to this body of work.

A paragraph should usually advance a point and use sources to support or qualify it, rather than giving one isolated paragraph to each article.

## 5. State the research gap precisely

A defensible gap statement identifies the unresolved issue and supports it with the review. Possible gap types include:
- an empirical uncertainty or inconsistent finding;
- a context or population that has not been adequately studied;
- a conceptual ambiguity;
- a methodological limitation in prior work;
- a practical or policy question for which current evidence is insufficient.

Do not claim that “no studies exist” unless the search was sufficiently rigorous to support that statement. Prefer a bounded formulation, such as “the studies reviewed provide limited evidence about…” when that is what the search supports.

## 6. Connect the review to the design

The literature review should help justify the questions, define concepts, identify useful measures or methods, inform the framework, and establish the intended contribution. Avoid promising that a study will “fill a gap” if its design cannot reasonably address the gap.

## 7. Reference integrity

Verify author names, title, year, journal or publisher, volume, issue, pages, and DOI or URL where relevant. Never create citations to make an argument look stronger. If a source cannot be checked, mark it as unverified and do not treat it as confirmed evidence.

## Reference for broader research-design guidance
