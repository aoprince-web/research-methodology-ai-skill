# Quantitative Methods Planning Guide

This guide supports planning and reviewing quantitative studies. Specific procedures depend on the research question, design, measurement, sampling, and disciplinary standards.

## 1. Identify the purpose

Clarify whether the study is intended to describe a population or phenomenon, estimate prevalence or levels, examine associations, compare groups, predict outcomes, evaluate a programme, or estimate a causal effect. The purpose should guide the design and analysis.

## 2. Define the unit and population

Specify the unit of analysis (such as a person, household, organisation, event, or record), target population, accessible population where relevant, sampling frame, and eligibility criteria. Avoid treating these terms as interchangeable.

## 3. Operationalise concepts

For each important construct or variable, record:
- conceptual definition;
- operational definition;
- measurement scale and coding;
- data source or instrument;
- timing of measurement;
- known limitations or likely measurement error.

If an existing scale is used, check its source, intended population, permissions, scoring rules, language, and evidence of suitability. Do not assume that a measure validated in one setting is automatically appropriate in another.

## 4. Select a design

- **Descriptive:** characterises a distribution or group at a defined time or period.
- **Cross-sectional:** measures variables within a defined period, often to estimate prevalence or associations.
- **Longitudinal:** examines change or patterns over time.
- **Correlational or observational:** examines relationships without assigning an intervention.
- **Quasi-experimental:** evaluates an intervention or exposure without full random assignment, using a design-specific strategy to address alternative explanations.
- **Experimental:** assigns an intervention or condition according to a specified allocation procedure, often randomisation.

The design label is not sufficient. Explain the sampling, timing, comparison, and analysis that make the design appropriate.

## 5. Sampling and sample-size rationale

State the intended sampling strategy and how participants or units will be selected. Probability sampling supports design-based population inference when properly implemented; non-probability sampling requires caution about representativeness and generalisation.

A sample-size rationale may depend on the primary outcome, effect or precision target, variability, significance level, power, design effect, expected non-response, and analysis. The relevant inputs differ by study. Do not provide a definitive number without stating the assumptions and calculation method. A convenience sample does not become representative merely because it is large.

## 6. Data quality and analysis

Plan data coding, range and consistency checks, missing-data handling, outlier review, assumptions, model choice, and any sensitivity analyses. Match the analysis to the question, measurement, design, and sampling structure.

Distinguish:
- descriptive estimates from inferential estimates;
- association from causation;
- statistical significance from practical importance;
- point estimates from their uncertainty;
- planned analysis from exploratory analysis.

For causal questions, consider the design's ability to address confounding, selection, temporal order, and other alternative explanations. Statistical adjustment alone does not guarantee causal identification.

## 7. Reporting

Report enough detail for readers to understand how data were collected, how variables were defined, how the sample was obtained, what analyses were performed, and what limitations affect interpretation. Do not invent missing values or imply that unperformed checks were completed.
