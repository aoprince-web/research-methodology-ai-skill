# Worked Research Design Examples

These examples are illustrative teaching scenarios, not reports of completed studies or evidence about actual populations. They show how the research question should guide design choices. Adapt them to the real context, literature, access, and ethics requirements.

## Example 1: Understanding students' experiences of academic stress

**Initial topic:** Academic stress among university students.

**Problem to resolve:** “Academic stress” is broad. The researcher needs to decide whether the focus is prevalence, associated factors, lived experience, coping, or institutional support.

**Possible qualitative direction:** “How do first-year students describe and make sense of academic stress during their transition to university?”

**Possible design:** A basic qualitative study, if the aim is to understand participants' interpretations and experiences without claiming a specialised phenomenological or grounded-theory tradition.

**Possible evidence:** Semi-structured interviews with students who meet defined inclusion criteria, supported by contextual notes.

**Analysis plan:** A clearly described coding and theme-development process suited to the selected analytic approach, including attention to contrasting accounts.

**Feasibility and ethics:** Consider recruitment through appropriate channels, the possibility that students may feel pressured if recruitment is linked to teaching staff, confidentiality in a small cohort, and referral information if sensitive issues arise.

**Claim boundary:** Findings could provide contextual insight into participants' accounts. They would not estimate how common academic stress is among all students or prove its causes.

## Example 2: Estimating the association between study habits and academic performance

**Possible question:** “What is the association between self-reported study habits and semester grades among eligible students in a defined institution?”

**Possible design:** A quantitative observational study, with the specific design determined by the timing and availability of data.

**Evidence:** A defined measure of study habits and a clearly specified academic-performance measure, with appropriate permissions and privacy protections.

**Analysis:** Descriptive statistics and a suitable association model chosen after examining measurement scales, distribution, sampling, and relevant assumptions.

**Key cautions:** Self-report measurement error, selection into the sample, missing data, and potential confounding factors. An observed association would not by itself establish that study habits caused differences in grades.

## Example 3: Explaining a survey pattern with interviews

**Possible first-stage question:** “Which barriers do respondents report most often when accessing a service?”

A survey may describe patterns, but it may not explain why particular barriers occur or how people experience them.

**Possible explanatory sequential plan:**
1. Conduct and analyse a feasible quantitative survey.
2. Identify patterns that need further explanation.
3. Select interview participants or cases using a transparent rationale linked to those patterns.
4. Conduct and analyse qualitative interviews.
5. Integrate the findings by showing which accounts explain, elaborate on, or complicate the survey results.

**Integration question:** What will the researcher do if interview accounts do not fit the survey pattern?

**Feasibility caution:** The second stage depends on the first stage. The schedule must include analysis between stages and enough time for recruitment and integration.

## What the examples demonstrate

- A broad topic must be refined before a design can be justified.
- The same topic can support different studies with different questions.
- Data collection techniques do not, by themselves, determine the research design.
- A method must be matched to the kind of evidence and claim required.
- Feasibility, ethics, and limitations belong in the design from the beginning.
