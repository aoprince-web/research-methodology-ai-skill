# Research Proposal Planning Template

Use this template to plan a study before drafting full academic prose. Adapt or remove sections to match the discipline, institution, supervisor, funder, or journal requirements. Not every project needs every section.

## 1. Working title

Write a concise title that indicates the main topic, population or case, and setting where appropriate. Avoid promising more than the study can establish.

## 2. Background and context

- What is the topic and why does it matter?
- What is known from credible literature or relevant data?
- What context does the reader need to understand the problem?
- Which claims require citations or verification?

## 3. Problem statement

Describe the specific issue, uncertainty, or unresolved question the study will address. Distinguish the practical problem from the scholarly gap. Support the gap with evidence from the literature rather than assuming that a lack of familiarity means a lack of research.

## 4. Purpose and contribution

**Purpose statement:** What will the study seek to understand, describe, explain, compare, evaluate, or develop?

**Potential contribution:** What could the study add to knowledge, practice, policy, or future research? Keep the claim proportionate to the proposed scope.

## 5. Aim and objectives

**Overall aim:** [One clear statement]

**Specific objectives:**
1. [Objective 1]
2. [Objective 2]
3. [Objective 3]

Each objective should be specific enough to guide evidence collection and analysis. Avoid objectives that are only activities, such as “to distribute questionnaires”, unless the assignment explicitly requires activity objectives.

## 6. Research questions and hypotheses

**Research questions**
1. [Question 1]
2. [Question 2]
3. [Question 3]

**Hypotheses, if justified by the design:** [State testable hypotheses or explain why hypotheses are not appropriate.]

Ensure every question follows from the problem and purpose and has a plausible answer through the proposed data and analysis.

## 7. Literature review themes

Organise the review around themes, concepts, findings, methodological debates, and relevant contexts. For each theme, note:
- key concepts and definitions;
- major findings and areas of agreement or disagreement;
- methods and contexts of prior studies;
- limitations or unresolved issues;
- relevance to the proposed study.

- Main concepts:
- Proposed relationships or assumptions:
- Why the framework fits this study:
- How it will inform the questions, data collection, or analysis:

If no framework is required or appropriate, explain the reasoning rather than adding one decoratively.

## 9. Methodology

### Research approach and design

State whether the study is qualitative, quantitative, or mixed methods and identify the specific design. Explain why it fits the questions and what claims it can support.

### Setting, population, case, or unit of analysis

Define the study setting and the population, case, organisation, document set, or unit being examined.

### Sampling and recruitment

- Target population or case-selection criteria:
- Sampling strategy:
- Proposed sample or number of cases:
- Recruitment or access route:
- Rationale for adequacy:
- Likely selection limitations:

Treat sample size as a reasoned proposal, not an unsupported guarantee.

### Data sources and collection

Describe the data needed for each research question, the collection tools or protocols, the procedure, language, timing, piloting where relevant, and permissions required.

### Data analysis

Explain the steps that will turn the data into findings. Link each planned analysis to a research question and specify software only where relevant. Do not promise analyses that the data cannot support.

### Quality and rigour

Identify relevant strategies, such as validity and reliability considerations for quantitative work, trustworthiness and reflexivity for qualitative work, or quality within each strand and integration for mixed methods.

## 10. Ethics and data management

Address consent, voluntary participation, risk, privacy, confidentiality, data storage, access, retention, destruction, permissions, and institutional review as relevant. State the approval status accurately. Do not imply approval has been granted before it has.

## 11. Scope and limitations

Define what the study will and will not cover. Identify foreseeable limits in access, measurement, sampling, time, transferability, generalisability, or causal inference.

Adapt the timeline to the real project duration. Include approval and recruitment time rather than assuming data collection can begin immediately.

## 13. References

List only sources actually cited in the proposal. Apply the required citation style and verify bibliographic details against reliable records or original publications.

## Final alignment check

- Can each objective be answered by one or more research questions?
- Is there a credible source of evidence for each question?
- Does the sampling or case-selection strategy fit the purpose?
- Can the planned analysis answer the questions?
- Are access, time, skills, budget, and ethics requirements realistic?
- Are all important claims supported by sources?
